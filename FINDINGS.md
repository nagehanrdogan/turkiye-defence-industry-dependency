# Progress Notes: Component-Level Dependency Analysis of Turkish Defence Platforms

**Status: ongoing PhD dissertation fieldwork (2026–present). This is a working note on methodology and interim findings, not a finished paper — it will be updated as the verification work continues.**

## Research question

SIPRI's Arms Transfers Database records platform-level transfers and flags "local production," but it does not link a locally-produced platform to the specific foreign subsystems (engines, sensors, naval weapons, etc.) it depends on. This project asks: for Turkiye's major defence platforms, which foreign components/suppliers is "local production" actually built on, and how has that dependency structure changed over time?

## Method

Dependency claims are cross-validated across three independent source types rather than relying on any single one:

1. **SIPRI Arms Transfers Database** — used both for its "local production" flag and, experimentally, for temporal co-occurrence between a platform's delivery years and other imports in the same period.
2. **Official export-control records** (US DSCA Major Arms Sales notifications, German *Rüstungsexportbericht*) — used where SIPRI coverage is thin or below its reporting threshold.
3. **Secondary/open-source literature** — principally Egeli, Güvenç, Kurç & Mevlütoğlu (2024), *"From Client to Competitor: The Rise of Türkiye's Defence Industry,"* IISS/CFPPR, and manufacturer/press reporting (company press releases, Naval News, Defence Turkey, Jamestown Foundation, etc.).

**A conceptual distinction worth stating explicitly:** this dataset tracks *component/subsystem dependency* — which foreign part or supplier a platform currently sources from. A separate, related form of dependency is not separately coded: *platform-origin export-control dependency*, the veto power an origin country retains over a platform's use, transfer, or modification by virtue of having supplied the base design, type certificate, or a component subject to its own export-control regime, independent of how much local production value has since been added. Germany's 2016–2021 arms embargo cutting off the Altay tank's original MTU/RENK powerpack, and the persistent US export-control interest implicit in third-country F-4E modernization programmes, both illustrate this second, distinct form of dependency. A starker case is Pakistan's cancelled 2018 order for 30 T129 ATAK helicopters: the US withheld an export licence for the LHTEC T800-4A engine (a Honeywell/Rolls-Royce joint venture), and rather than forcing a supplier substitution as with Altay, this blocked the transfer outright — Pakistan eventually turned to a Chinese alternative instead. This case never appears in the SIPRI-derived dataset, since SIPRI records deliveries rather than cancelled orders — a reminder that this kind of dependency can be invisible in transfer-flow data precisely because its effect is to prevent a transfer from happening at all.

**A methodological result worth flagging on its own:** an early version of this project tried to establish supplier links purely statistically — matching a platform's delivery year against other imports arriving in the same window. Run end-to-end against the 36 SIPRI-flagged "local production" designs (script and full write-up in [`criticality_matrix/README.md`](criticality_matrix/README.md)), this heuristic returns a candidate subsystem match for 36 of 36 (100%) — i.e., it fails to discriminate at all. That negative result is treated as a finding in its own right: temporal co-occurrence alone is not usable evidence for platform–subsystem linkage in this dataset, and every dependency claim in the underlying dataset is now instead confirmed through the multi-source process above, with unconfirmed cases explicitly labelled as such rather than silently dropped or asserted.

## A second negative result: the criticality-scoring layer was tried and abandoned

The "next phase" originally planned here — a criticality-scoring layer (functional necessity × export restriction × substitution availability, adapted from the EU's Critical Raw Materials methodology, Blengini et al. 2017) — was piloted on six cases (T-70, T129, Altay, F-4E, SOM/SOM-J, Anka-3) and abandoned on 2026-08-05. Three separate proxies were tried for the "functional necessity" axis and each failed for a distinct reason: programme-level budget (SSB's own official reporting has no platform-level budget breakdown to draw on), count of how many other platforms share a supplier (inflated by conglomerates like Lockheed Martin or IAI matching unrelated product lines, and structurally zero for genuinely new dependencies that haven't yet recurred elsewhere, which understates rather than reflects their risk), and an FMEA/Severity-style engineering framing (reduced to a plain operational test — can the platform perform its role without this component — but even in that simplified form the dataset didn't support confident scoring across the full set). Consistent with this project's practice of documenting negative results rather than quietly dropping them, this is recorded as a finding: the committee's stated expectation — that the dataset explains the questions it problematizes — is already met by the verified dependency mapping and the EBG pilot study, without needing a separate scoring instrument, and building one on impressionistic ratings would have broken from the two-source verification standard the rest of this project holds itself to.

## A third methodological result: an aggregate accuracy figure can hide which half of a dataset is usable

A supporting exercise required classifying the ~2,400 products in the Presidency of Defence Industries' 2026 Product Catalogue (a 1,366-page PDF, published in parallel English and Turkish editions) against the catalogue's own 16-category "fields of activity" taxonomy. Three things from that pass are worth recording as method rather than as substantive findings.

First, on extraction: an initial version detected product entries with a text heuristic ("short all-caps line"), which left 31% of products unclassifiable. The catalogue turns out to be typographically rigid — product titles are always 14pt bold, body text always 9pt light — and keying extraction to that structural property instead of to the text pattern both made the parse deterministic and, more importantly, brought the product *description* paragraphs into the dataset for the first time. Unclassified fell from 31% to 3.4%. The parallel-language editions then provided a free validation channel: the two editions are page-for-page mirrors, and independently extracting both yielded matching product counts for 145 of 153 companies (~0.2% divergence overall). A related caution: the earlier pass had recorded seven companies as extraction failures because no products were detected for them; re-checking against the source showed most of those companies genuinely have only a profile page and no product entries, so part of what looked like a parser bug was a real property of the source.

Second, and the substantive methodological point: a hand-audited random sample put the classifier's overall accuracy at roughly 75%, but that single number turned out to be close to meaningless on its own. Splitting the audit by *how* each row had been matched showed rows matched on the product title running at ~91% accuracy and rows matched only on description text at ~53% — two populations of comparable size (54%/43% of the dataset) with entirely different evidentiary value, averaged into one figure that described neither. The response was not to tune the classifier further but to publish the match provenance alongside every row as a confidence flag, so that quantitative claims can be restricted to the high-confidence subset and the remainder is available for exploration without being citable. The generalisable lesson is that for a heuristic labelling step, *how* a label was arrived at can be a better predictor of whether to trust it than any global accuracy score, and reporting only the aggregate would have overstated the weak half and understated the strong one.

Third, a small transferable rule that fixed a whole error class: terms naming a host platform ("naval," "aircraft," "armoured vehicle") describe where a product is *used*, not what it *is*. Counting them as evidence when they appeared in description text was systematically misfiling components by their deployment context — a thermal camera "for naval vessels" classified as a naval platform. Restricting such terms to title matches only removed the error class outright.

As elsewhere in this project, this classification is labelled as an estimate rather than an authority: the catalogue conveys its own category assignments as icons, which are not recoverable from the document text, so these labels are inferred and are not the publisher's own tagging.
